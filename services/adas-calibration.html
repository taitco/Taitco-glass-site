What ADAS Actually Is

ADAS stands for Advanced Driver Assistance Systems — the umbrella term for the safety features that have quietly become standard on most new vehicles: automatic emergency braking, lane departure warnings, blind-spot monitoring, adaptive cruise control, and more. If your car was built in the last decade or so, there's a good chance it has at least one of these systems running.

None of it works without cameras, radar, and sensors mounted at exact points around your vehicle, all aimed with precision. Calibration is what keeps that aim correct. Think of the safety features as the brain — calibration is what makes sure the eyes and ears are actually pointed where they need to be.

Why a Degree Off Matters

A forward-facing camera aimed just one degree off its intended target can put a full foot of error into its read at 60 feet — and that error keeps growing with distance. At highway range, a degree of misalignment can mean the difference between a system correctly spotting a vehicle ahead and placing it somewhere it isn't.

Here's the part most people don't expect: your dashboard usually won't tell you. A misaligned sensor rarely trips a warning light. The system can look completely normal while quietly reading the road wrong. Calibration is the only way to actually know it's aimed right.

Common Questions

ADAS Calibration FAQ

What is ADAS, and does my vehicle have it?

Advanced Driver Assistance Systems — safety features like automatic emergency braking, lane departure warnings, blind-spot monitoring, and adaptive cruise control. Most vehicles from the last decade have at least one. We'll confirm what's on yours when you book.

What does calibration actually mean?

It's the process of aligning the cameras and sensors that feed your safety systems, so they're pointed exactly where the manufacturer intends. Without it, those systems can be running on bad information.

Do I need calibration if I'm not getting a new windshield?

Possibly. Wheel alignments, tire changes, collision repairs, suspension work, mirror replacement, and bumper work can all shift a sensor's aim. If you're not sure, ask us and we'll tell you straight.

Will my dashboard warn me if a sensor is off?

Not reliably. A misaligned sensor often won't trigger any warning light — the system can appear completely normal while it's actually reading the road wrong.

Does insurance cover calibration?

Often, yes — especially as part of a covered collision or windshield replacement claim. Coverage varies by policy, so we'll help you check before the work starts.

How long does it take?

Static calibration typically runs 30 minutes to an hour per system. Dynamic calibration, which requires driving the vehicle, can take longer. Some vehicles need both — we'll give you a real estimate once we know what your vehicle requires.

What happens if I skip it?

The car will still drive fine. But the safety systems relying on that sensor may not perform the way they're supposed to — and there's often no warning that anything's wrong. If the vehicle changes hands later, the next owner has no way of knowing either.

Is calibration included with a windshield replacement?

Yes — when your vehicle requires it, it's included as part of the job. Not a surprise line item.
